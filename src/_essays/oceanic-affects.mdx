Cannabis enjoys a reputation for being a cuddly, easy-to-treat-casually drug among its users and non-users alike. It is indebted to the fact that many go through a phase of bong-rips, vape-hits, and chewable gummies in their teens and twenties, only to moderate or abandon it at the onset of life's real responsibilities. This is undeserved. For me and many others, it is a substance that is prone to abuse and catastrophic psychological fallout. Its embrace is acidic: warm at first, but damn if it isn't painful after a while.

Anyone convinced by the drug's reputation should consider my experiences with it. Two multi-week stays in a psych ward, believing that everyone there were federal agents plotting to frame me for various crimes. Tearing up the linoleum floor tiles of one of my psych ward rooms, looking for hidden messages underneath. Thinking thoughts so alien and disturbing, so non-native to my own brain and personality that I thought they had to be the result of microscopic machines injected along with my COVID-19 vaccine. Lying repeatedly to the people closest to me, causing damage to our relationships that I am still repairing. Trying to escape my own mind by ending it all via overdose of blood-pressure-lowering beta-blockers. Not so cuddly.

Having had such experiences with cannabis, and yet finding prohibition to be unfavorable due to the harsh penalties imposed on the convicted, I find myself in the uncomfortable position of wishing it were legal while being terrified of the potential consequences that a sunsetting taboo may have on the psychologically vulnerable. One wishes that education and mental health screening were adequate for addressing the question of cannabis, but everyone must be familiar with the ease with which one can ignore warning cues in the pursuit of sensory gratification. Consider my disclosure here to be something of mild warning to the reader. Be careful; it can sneak up on you.

But so I owe to the despair of my addictive episodes and withdrawals a new understanding of the anxiolytic effects of prayer and meditation and a new desire for a more rigid moral framework than vague moral intuition developed by osmosis could provide. To be sure, I know many people who are not religious and yet still act in a saint-like manner. In pursuing my substance of choice over everything, even core values like honesty and integrity, I cannot say that atheism was the cause of my troubles. But the saintly atheists are exceptional. Most people would benefit from engaging with the concept of a higher power. I can no longer count myself as being outside that majority.

Had I been asked at any point during the last fifteen years if I were religious, I would have answered resolutely in the negative. Even during sojourns in the beautiful and chaotic worlds of LSD and psilocybin — where patterns emerge from nowhere and interlock and dance and breath and where one becomes acquainted with the wild beauty and meaning in the most laughable, insignificant-in-the-grand-scheme things — experiences of the numinous were rare and nothing was felt beyond a sense of marvel at the profoundly weird hallucinations, the time-warps, and the intense appreciation for art of all kinds that those drugs can provide.

But now, having run up against my own very human limitations, I must say that I have become a more religious person, albeit one who doubts the Resurrection and all miracles and who cannot bring himself not to ultimately be agnostic as to whether God actually exists, anyway, the question seeming to me to be kind of moot, considering that He has thankfully not revealed Himself and that all of religions’ positive effects seem to operate without any such revelation being necessary. Prayer and the story of Christ have become important. Meditation and the Middle Path have become important. And these things’ becoming important has begotten a turning within me from an atheistic worldview to a more-so-but-not-quite-fully-religious one, on the basis of which I feel perfectly justified in calling myself both Christian and Buddhist, despite the apparent contradictions and even if an ultimate, literal belief proves impossible.

The turn I have taken toward religion was not begotten by any reevaluation of scriptural claims to historicity; I still believe that any truth once represented in our religious texts has long since been obscured by mutation and embellishment, a process that owes its drive to human beings' tendency to manipulate the truth, either consciously or not. Our religions are full of lies: the miracles did not occur. The Resurrection did not occur.

But it is precisely this selective manipulation of the truth that has delivered to us a set of world religions whose beliefs and practices have such a high degree of psychological salience. People repeat those aspects of a story or practice that are most gripping and effective, and this happens iteratively, generation after generation, and so then we can safely say that what we have is an evolutionary process that appears to be aiming for psychological potency in much the same way that biological evolution appears to be aiming for fitness.

This line of thinking became meaningful to me only after I had abandoned pure atheism and started to flirt with embracing at least some aspects of religiosity. After experiencing my traumatic drug addiction, I had to abandon the atheist's tendency to prize absolute truth in order to fix some part of myself that I had damaged in the pursuit of earthly, material pleasure. As I suffered my way through psychotic withdrawals, I could no longer ignore the anxiolytic effects of prayer and meditation.

We, the people who require a God, do so because we fail in confrontation with the multiplicity of ethical outcomes that arise from any given decision, and the higher power is an assistant in navigating a world of those endlessly complex decisions. From each moral decision taken stems a cascade of consequences, each requiring more decisions and yielding yet more consequences and requiring yet more decisions, etc., the final results of which we can be only partially aware. The saintly atheist has no problem choosing the actions that beget the best of these consequences without prayer and the eternal observer. I suspect this is due to a superior psychological makeup. But, as a pure atheist, I have demonstrated that I do not elicit the best of outcomes — not by any means.

I have a tendency, which I have also noticed in my fellow humans, to be not cruel but often indifferent toward others, often even those closest and most loving toward me. Religion is an antidote to this inadequacy. Everyone must be familiar with the phenomenon that one behaves differently when alone and when around different people. Observers demonstrably affect our behavior in accordance with the characteristics of those observers, and so for those of us who do better with religion in our lives, we must owe it to the imposition of the constant presence of an eternal, ultimately moral observer. This eternal observer must owe its beneficial effects to the facts that it reduces the variance of one's behavior and that it moves the curve to the positive end of the distribution.

When I write of God in this way, I do not mean to imply that I feel His presence. For me He is simply a hypothetical entity with which to have a moral dialogue — not knowably real in the same way an object or another person is, but real enough as a concept to get the job done. This attitude might be called prayerful agnosticism. The dialogue that one has with God both maintains the illusion of His presence and it serves to make moral reasoning linguistic and therefore explicit. This has the effect of pulling from pre-linguistic liminal consciousness the thoughts that eventually become actions. And so by describing one's actions and desires, they take on a starker definition that leaves them more open to scrutiny. This is an aid to the habitually amoral.

I have so far said nothing of why I have chosen Christianity and the Abrahamic God as the anchors of my moral worldview. The truth of it is that this choice was made for me when I was born into a Western culture with Christian moral underpinnings. To the extent that I am educated about Christ's example, I find that it comports well with what goodness already means to me, and this is undoubtedly due to the Christian cultural context of my upbringing. What of the other world religions? If I imagine myself to have been born into a Hindu or Muslim context, I cannot help but think that I would turn to some aspect of those religions when the interactions of my genes with my environment would inevitably have led me to similar crises as those that made me turn to Christian ideas. I make no claim for the superiority of Christianity, and I would advise anyone in a place of desperation to turn to whichever religion or spiritual system is most salient to them.

In fact — and despite my wholly Western upbringing — there are aspects of Buddhism that have proven useful to me during my crises. I am referring to what is in the West popularly called mindfulness meditation and to the Buddhist concept of the Middle Path; meditation being a practice that has anxiolytic, mind-mending effects in common with prayer because both are a deliberate exercise that involves training one's attention on something material — what is more vital and grounding than our breath and voice? — and the Middle Path being the idea that one should act in accordance with the idea of living in the area between asceticism and sensory gratification. But just as with prayer and God-as-a-concept, I do not find it necessary to hold any ultimate beliefs about Buddhist enlightenment in order to receive benefit from this practice. I only need to interact with whichever are the most salient and useful concepts the religion provides.

Given this endorsement of other spiritualisms and my commitment to an agnostic posture toward religion, it may seem that my beliefs are weak. And it is true that I developed this argument exactly for the purposes of benefiting from religious ideas and practices while assuaging the rebellion of my atheistic heart, wanting always to have my cake and eat it too. But all this entails is that there are two kinds of belief, that of ultimate truth and that of practical utility. These ways of believing can — and always do — occur within the same person. Does God exist? Is there such a thing as spiritual enlightenment? Can't claim to know.

But these concepts have become undeniable, no profound spiritual experience required. They have benefits that the desperate cannot afford to deny, if they are willing to go through the mere motions of doing something that doesn't quite make sense. Why hold a conversation with something that isn't there? And why sit and concentrate only on your breath for half an hour each day? It doesn't matter; it works, and we can at once sift through religions' offerings — searching not for absolution or a ticket to heaven but for those gem ideas that can truly help us in times of need — while also reserving judgement about the ultimate truth in an attitude of humility and grace.

Even after recovery from the drug-induced psychosis and a rehab stint and my indecisive soul-searching, sobriety remains a difficult, one-day-at-a-time effort. I am committed, but commitment is weak to the addict's impulsivity. It remains unclear whether sobriety is maintainable and that unclarity will remain with me for the rest of my life. Such is the fog of war. But there are aids available, psychological tools derived from our most ancient spiritualisms which point the way. All that remains is to pay attention to them.
